# Market and gap survey

Seven parallel surveys of the competitive landscape, the England statutory
curriculum, accessibility law, the computing-education literature, UK teacher
adoption, design craft and simulation environments. Every claim below was
checked against a source the surveyor actually opened.

## Where Kodro actually stands

Kodro does not lead on any single axis and should stop trying to. Scratch, ScratchJr, Blockly Games and micro:bit MakeCode already ship free, offline, no-account, and they beat Kodro on ecosystem, polish and KS1 age-fit; ScratchJr is better tuned for ages 5-7 than a Python subset ever will be. Webots and Gazebo beat it on physics by a margin one developer cannot close. NCCE Teach Computing already gives English teachers 535 free, DfE-mapped lessons against Kodro's 24, and VEX already publishes named England Computing programme-of-study alignment, so "maps to the DfE PoS" is not a differentiator and should not be claimed as one. CoderZ does 3D physics plus text Python more convincingly, at $2,400/year, cloud-only, capped at 240 students outside the US. What nobody occupies is the conjunction: a 3D physics-simulated robot, pupil-written text Python, automatic marking of the specific run the pupil watched against per-lesson DfE and BCS criteria, at zero cost, zero accounts, zero network, on a locked-down school Chromebook. The nearest thing to it, VEXcode VR, has no Python at all on its free tier and its offline client requires a $199+/year licence plus an internet licence check every 30 days. That empty quadrant is real and defensible, but it is narrower than the current claim: the lesson library is 3 KS1, 4 KS2, 9 KS3 and 8 KS4, measured on 15 August 2026 by reading the `key_stage` field of all 24 files in `src/robolearn/lessons/library`. This sentence originally read "1 KS1, 2 KS2, 8 KS3, 7 KS4" over an 18-lesson library and concluded that "Key Stage 1 to 4" was the most falsifiable sentence in the project and should be narrowed to "upper KS2 through KS4, with a KS1 taster". The lower end was thickened afterwards and that recommendation was overtaken: `Kodro_Dissertation.tex:336` now states the measured split and says the KS1 floor went from one taster to three lessons. The survey's underlying point survives in weaker form. The library is still densest across KS3 and KS4 and thinnest at KS1, so "Key Stage 1 to 4" describes a usable entry path rather than even coverage, and should not be presented as even coverage. The strongest argument for the architecture is not novelty, it is survival: Replit killed Teams for Education in 2023 explicitly because free education hosting did not pay, Google shut CS First on 30 June 2025 and deleted all user data, Robot Virtual Worlds broke in 2021 when AWS deprecated a protocol its backend depended on, and Trinket, the default embedded Python widget across UK KS3/GCSE resources, goes dark on 31 August 2026, eleven days before this dissertation is due. Kodro is structurally immune to every one of those failures, and that is the thesis.

### 1. Predict-then-run and a step-through replay of the recorded trace
*engine, multi-day*

**DELIVERED. Closed after the survey was written; verified 15 August 2026.** Both halves ship. The trace stepper: `hooks.jsx` records `runTraceRef`, a per-step log of what the program did with the rover's state after each step, capped at `TRACE_CAP = 600`, written by `traceStep(desc, line, vars)` during the watched run from the same live state the grade reads, so the trace cannot disagree with the mark. `app.jsx:2550-2553` opens it (`setStepIdx(0); setStepperOpen(true)`), highlights the traced line in the editor via `setActiveLine`, and labels the control `aria-label="Step through the last run, one instruction at a time"`. The prediction: `app.jsx` carries `predictArmedRef`, `predictText`, `predictResult` and `predictionRunStateRef`, the last of which captures the prediction at launch specifically so that editing it mid-run cannot change what was predicted. The recorded prediction is then rendered next to the measured outcome (`app.jsx:3356`, and in the exported evidence table at `app.jsx:2488`, whose column header is `Pre-run prediction`).

**What a user could not do when this survey was written.** A pupil could not record a prediction before running, could not step the program one instruction at a time, and could not see rover state (x, y, heading, battery, samples) at a chosen step. Every lesson was write-run-be-marked, so there was no way to practise reading code and no way to build a trace table. 00_first_drive.yaml cites the DfE KS1 statement 'use logical reasoning to predict the behaviour of simple programs' while the product then contained no mechanism by which a pupil ever recorded a prediction. The original wording said "every one of the 18 lessons"; the library is 24 as at 15 August 2026.

**Evidence.** Pedagogy review: Predict was the most-mentioned PRIMM stage across nine teacher interviews (28% of coded segments) and is entirely absent from Kodro; grep for 'predict' in web/app.py hits only Ollama's num_predict. Lister/Venables' ~50% tracing-accuracy-before-writing threshold is attested verbatim in both the RPF review s1.6 and Sentance et al. s2.3.2. Curriculum review: AQA 8525 s3.1.1 explicitly requires pupils to 'use trace tables and visual inspection to determine how simple algorithms work'. The data already exists: runtime/tracer.py records every event, grader.py consumes it via _compute_aggregates, and the payload is deliberately JSON-friendly, so this is a replay of data Kodro already crosses the web boundary with, not a new interpreter mode.

**What a user could not do when this survey was written.** A six-year-old on lesson 00 who typed move_forward(3 saw 'syntax: SyntaxError: <CPython wording> (line 1)', with no reading-age adaptation despite every lesson YAML carrying a reading_age field. The surface is finite because the sandbox already bans imports, f-strings, dicts and input, so roughly 15-25 (exception type, message pattern) entries cover it, and executor.py already emits pupil-voiced sandbox violations via first.message() so half the plumbing exists. Frame it honestly as an age-appropriateness intervention, since Denny et al. 2014 found message enhancement ineffectual in a university context.

### 3. Work that survives the end of the lesson on a managed Chromebook
*engine, a day*

**PARTLY DELIVERED; verified 15 August 2026.** The warning half ships. `app.jsx:1856-1860` carries the interstitial this entry asked for, and its own comment states the case in the same terms: "everything here lives in localStorage, shared and ephemeral profiles wipe it at sign-out, and the pupil finds out next lesson. **What a user could not do when this survey was written.** A pupil designs a robot in period 3, signs out of a shared or ephemeral Chrome profile, and the work is gone. **Evidence.** Teacher-adoption review, verified against Google's own Chrome Enterprise doc (support.google.com/chrome/a/answer/3538894): on exit from ephemeral mode 'the local browser profile gets removed... this includes cached browsing data, localStorage, and downloaded files'; enrolled shared devices also purge profiles oldest-first to free 2GB. The fix is cheap because the .kodro export/import path already exists (app.jsx:1100, file input at app.jsx:1957 accepting .kodro/.json), so the work is promoting it to the canonical save: an end-of-lesson save interstitial, a first-run ephemeral/incognito warning, File System Access API writes to the pupil's network home drive where available, and resume-from-file on load.

### 6. A non-visual channel for the run: aria-live narration log plus informational sonification
*accessibility, a day*

**PARTLY DELIVERED; verified 15 August 2026.** The narration half ships. `app.jsx:721-737` defines a `narration` live region and a `narrate(text)` function carrying the moments during a run: a collision as it happens, a sample as it is picked up, an error at its line. The end-of-run summary has its own separate region, `runAnnouncement`, and the comment states the two never fire together. One detail is worth keeping: `narrate` appends a zero-width character keyed to an incrementing counter, because a screen reader only re-reads a live region when its content changes and two identical events in a row would otherwise be silent the second time. A head of computing cannot answer the SENCO's question in advance, which is exactly what the Equality Act anticipatory duty asks.

**Evidence.** Accessibility review: the binding duty is the Equality Act 2010 anticipatory reasonable-adjustments duty including auxiliary aids (PSBAR does not apply, schools are exempt except for administrative functions). Milne & Ladner's Blocks4All (CHI 2018), children aged 5-10 programming a robot, names Accessing Output as barrier #1 and solved it with a tangible robot, a route Kodro's virtual rover closes. The Game Accessibility Guidelines independently name the same two missing items: an audio description track and a pingable sonar-style audio map. Kodro already has the substrate: sound.js is a full offline Web Audio synthesis engine with a motor loop that tracks the sim's speed profile, and it is muted by default (line 19). The work is one aria-live line per executed command plus an end-of-run spoken summary against each criterion, and promoting three cues in sound.js from decoration to information. Blocks4All's guidelines warn explicitly against a separate screen-reader-only mode, so keep one interface: the narration log doubles as the teacher's run transcript and as scaffolding for low reading age.

### 10. A floor raster and a down-facing colour/reflectance sensor
*engine, multi-day*

**What a user cannot do today.** All six worlds are flat planes with scattered obstacles and one forward distance sensor, which means Kodro cannot express line following, coloured zones, grid cells, swamps, holes or no-go paint. That is the single most-taught robotics task in the world and the closest competitor ships two eye sensors and a location sensor against Kodro's one rangefinder.

**Evidence.** Environments review: RoboCupJunior Rescue Line is 'a modular arena formed by tiles with different patterns'; the Erebus simulation rules define the world purely as tiles plus floor-typed hazards ('Swamps: brown areas that reduce robot speed', 'Black holes: cause robot to drop through'). VEX kb: 'The VR Robot has two Eye Sensors, one that faces forward, and another that faces down', plus a Location Sensor reporting (X, Y) and compass heading. This is not physics work: it is a per-world 2D raster (colour, reflectance, traversability) sampled at the robot's footprint each tick, plus a sensor primitive. It unlocks the largest number of new lessons per unit of engine effort, and a bang-bang versus proportional line controller is a genuine two-algorithm comparison for KS3.

### 14. Close the four cheap curriculum gaps and fix the one visible mapping overclaim
*content, a day*

**What a user cannot do today.** 04_selection.yaml cites the KS3 Boolean-logic statement while its allowed_constructs are [function_call, if, comparison] with no and/or/not, which is the one mapping an examiner checking a single claim will find. **Re-checked 15 August 2026: that overclaim is still live.** `src/robolearn/lessons/library/04_selection.yaml:24` still reads `allowed_constructs: [function_call, if, comparison]` with no and/or/not, so this remains the highest-value single fix in the entry. The debugging half, however, has been closed: `00d_fix_the_turn.yaml` and `04a_fix_the_condition.yaml` are both fault-finding lessons, and `00d_fix_the_turn` is the lesson the MCP grading demonstration uses precisely because it fails as authored and passes once fixed. The original wording, now superseded on that one point, said: there is also no debugging lesson (debugging is statutory at KS1 and KS2 and named in the DfE GCSE aims), no testing-with-normal-boundary-erroneous-data lesson, and no embedded-systems framing despite pupils literally choosing a controller board from real hobby parts. The testing and embedded-systems lessons are still absent.

**Evidence.** Curriculum review: DfE GCSE content requires 'Boolean logic using AND, OR and NOT, combinations of these' and AQA s3.2.5 names all three; interpreter.js already has evaluator cases for 'and', 'or', 'not' so a compound-condition lesson is content-only. AQA s3.2.12 requires testing 'using normal, boundary and erroneous test data', which has physical meaning in an arena (the sample at the exact edge, the obstacle at exactly the sensor threshold, a turn of exactly 360 degrees) and is a small extension of the existing success_criteria machinery. The DfE May 2024 proposal defines embedded systems as 'computer systems with specialised functions... typically not reprogrammable', the one systems-architecture topic a robot-design tool can claim credibly. Debugging fits a rover better than almost any other context because the failure is visible, and tracer.py already supports it. State the scope boundary explicitly too: Kodro covers roughly AQA s3.1 and s3.2 and none of s3.3-s3.8 (binary, data representation, CPU architecture, networks, cyber security, SQL, impacts).

### 15. Scalar constraint primitives: energy budget, run clock with a home base, comms delay, and an efficiency metric in the grader
*engine, a day*

**What a user cannot do today.** Every lesson is pass/fail against boolean criteria (grader.py: score = max(0, 100 - 20 * len(reasons)), so a five-criterion lesson can only score 100/80/60/40/20/0 and missing all three samples costs exactly what using 61% battery instead of 60% costs). There is no way to set a resource bound, no way to force offline planning, and no way to compare two solutions to the same arena, so the optimisation lesson cannot actually demonstrate that one algorithm is more efficient than another.

**Evidence.** Curriculum review: AQA s3.1.2 requires pupils to understand that more than one algorithm can solve the same problem and to 'compare the efficiency of algorithms', focusing on time efficiency; the sim already counts steps and time, so reporting a metric rather than a boolean delivers the statement literally. Environments review: an energy budget with no mid-run intervention is the honest framing for KS2's 'controlling or simulating physical systems' (Autosub Long Range vehicles run months-long missions under ice with no contact), a run clock plus fixed launch zone is what creates the optimisation problem in FLL-style missions, and a command delay queue is a one-line change that explains why a rover must be autonomous at all (the ExoMars Rosalind Franklin rover was built in Stevenage, which is a better UK hook than generic Mars). All four are scalar state on the existing kinematic engine, no new physics. The grader.py docstring is already honest that an earlier version promised a per-collision deduction the code never applied, so the scoring model is due a revisit anyway: consider dropping the numeric score from the pupil UI in favour of the criteria checklist, and emitting a confirmation reason for passed criteria, not just failed ones.

## Deliberately not chasing

- A block-based editor. Scratch, ScratchJr and Blockly own this outright, offline and free, with better age-fit and an ecosystem Kodro cannot approach. Parsons problems plus a click-to-insert command palette recover the two affordances the block literature names first (no typing, no memorising commands) for a fraction of the maintenance cost, and Kodro's differentiator is text Python, not another blocks product.
- Physics fidelity. Webots (Apache 2.0, EPFL, ROS/ROS2) and Gazebo already give real rigid-body dynamics, sensor noise models and arbitrary URDF/SDF robots, free and offline. Conceding this explicitly and early buys credibility; the counter-argument is that Gazebo needs an Ubuntu-class install (Windows runtime issues are flagged in its own docs), has no curriculum, no marking and no age-appropriate UI, and would never survive a school-managed image. Different products, different users.
- A full scheme of work. NCCE Teach Computing ships 535 free, DfE-funded, curriculum-mapped lessons with slides, worksheets, homework and assessments. Twenty-four lessons (18 when this survey was written) cannot compete on coverage, authorship or institutional trust. Position Kodro as the zero-cost simulator that drops into named TCC units (Y1 Moving a robot, Y2 Robot algorithms, Y5 Selection in physical computing, Y6 Sensing movement, Y9 Applying programming skills with physical computing) where the barrier today is buying micro:bit or Crumble hardware.
- Any account, cloud sync, hosted teacher dashboard or telemetry. This violates the hard constraints, and the survey found three documented deaths caused by exactly that dependency (Replit 2023, CS First 2025, Robot Virtual Worlds broken by an AWS protocol deprecation in 2021) plus Trinket going dark on 31 August 2026. The zero-server design is the dissertation's argument; adding a server would delete it.
- LLM-generated hints, feedback or error messages, including a local model. Beyond the no-cloud constraint, the evidence is against it: Leinonen et al. (UKICER 2024, n=106) found GPT-4-enhanced error messages beat conventional compiler messages in only 1 of 6 tasks, while handwritten expert explanations beat both. A hand-authored lookup table is the better product, not the poorer substitute.
- Liquid Glass, translucency and refraction effects. Chromium performs a backdrop texture readback per composited frame, the target device is a Celeron N4500 with integrated graphics, Microsoft's own docs say translucent material is GPU-intensive and auto-disables on low-end hardware, the W3C has no interoperable backdrop-refraction primitive (open SVG WG issue), and the BDA style guide says to avoid patterned or pictorial backgrounds behind text outright. Kodro should reach the right visual direction by deleting backdrop-filter, not by tuning it.
- True concurrent multi-robot simulation. Running N pupil programs under a deterministic interleaving, with robot-robot collision and per-program outcome attribution, is a milestone rather than a feature for a solo project. The 90% version is one pupil-controlled robot plus keyframed scripted movers, which gets yielding, reservation and deadlock onto the screen for the cost of a mover system that the pavement-delivery scenario needs anyway. Note that the sim league most like Kodro (Erebus) and RCJ Rescue Line and Maze are all single-robot.
- More worlds as reskins. Six flat planes with scattered obstacles across 17 sites is one world repeated 23 times. VEXcode VR, with the largest installed base, varies the task and the props on a near-identical playground rather than the scenery. Interaction verbs (floor raster, carry/release, scalar budgets, moving actors, a pen trail) buy far more lessons per unit of work than a seventh biome.
- Breadth across the rest of GCSE Computer Science. Binary and hex, data representation of text/sound/graphics, CPU architecture and fetch-decode-execute, networks and protocols, cyber security, databases and SQL, and the ethical/legal/environmental strand cannot be honestly taught by a rover in an arena. Say so: Kodro addresses roughly AQA s3.1 and s3.2, with embedded systems as the one credible carve-out from s3.4. Claiming otherwise is refutable in one page of a specification.
- Claiming to be first or only in mapping to the DfE programme of study. VEX already publishes England Computing programme-of-study alignment documents per STEM Lab. The claim that survives is the conjunction (offline, no account, 3D physics robot, pupil-written text Python, DfE plus BCS criteria, automatic marking of the observed run), never a superlative on any single axis.
- Recursive sorting staged with one rover. Linear versus binary search over numbered beacons and bubble sort as physical reordering are honest; merge sort's recursive split and merge is contrived with a single robot, and shipping a contrived lesson to satisfy a spec point undermines the credibility of the genuine mappings.
